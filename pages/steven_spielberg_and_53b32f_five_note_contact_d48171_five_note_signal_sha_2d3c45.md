## Introduction

The five-note phrase in *[Close Encounters]({{ 'close-encounters/' | relative_url }}) of the Third Kind* feels simple because it can be remembered after a single hearing. It feels alien because it never settles into the familiar patterns that listeners usually expect from a song. John Williams and Steven Spielberg deliberately searched for a musical shape that sat between a signal and a melody: structured enough to suggest intelligence, but incomplete enough to remain mysterious. Williams later explained that once a sequence grew beyond [five notes]({{ 'five-notes/' | relative_url }}) it started sounding like a tune rather than a communication signal. The final motif therefore occupies a rare middle ground, functioning as both message and enigma.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.pbs.org/wnet/gperf/john-williams-great-performances-theme-song-and-interview/4075/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pbs.org">[PBS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pbs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">John Williams&#x27; Great Performances Theme Song and...Some five notes. ## Between Noise and Melody

Most sounds are easy to categorise. Random noise feels accidental. A complete melody feels intentional and recognisably human. The *Close Encounters* motif succeeds because it occupies the territory between those two extremes.

Williams recalled composing hundreds of possible five-note combinations after Spielberg insisted that the contact signal should contain exactly five notes. He eventually concluded that fewer notes would be too fragmentary, while six or seven notes would begin to sound like a conventional melody. The chosen phrase therefore behaves more like a coded transmission than a piece of music.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.pbs.org/wnet/gperf/john-williams-great-performances-theme-song-and-interview/4075/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pbs.org">[PBS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pbs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">John Williams&#x27; Great Performances Theme Song and...Some five notes. That distinction matters psychologically. Human listeners instinctively search for patterns. When a sound contains enough structure to imply intention but not enough information to explain itself, the brain treats it as meaningful yet unresolved. The motif triggers recognition without providing complete understanding. In other words, it sounds as though somebody is trying to communicate, but not necessarily somebody human.

Spielberg himself reportedly described the motif as a kind of “doorbell” rather than a theme. A doorbell announces a presence without explaining who has arrived. ## Pattern Recognition Without a Full Tune

The phrase is built from large, slightly unexpected interval jumps rather than the smooth movement common in many popular melodies. Because the phrase is neither random nor conventionally melodic, audiences experience it as a pattern waiting to be decoded. That sensation mirrors the film's larger theme: encountering intelligence without sharing a language.

## Why Unfinished Music Invites Response

One of the most important features of the motif is that it does not sound complete.

Williams noted that the phrase effectively hangs on its final note, as though awaiting an answer. Rather than resolving itself, it leaves an open space. Spielberg's communication sequence then fills that space when the mothership responds with variations and replies.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://thelistenersclub.com/2020/01/03/john-williams-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-a-cinematic-tone-poem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelistenersclub.com">[The Listeners&#x27; Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelistenersclub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">john williams close encounters of the third kind a cinematic tone poem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Listeners&#x27; ClubJohn Williams&#x27; “Close Encounters of the Third Kind”3 Jan 2020 — Spielberg described the score&#x27;s iconic five-note “Comm...</span></span></span>

This creates a subtle but powerful dramatic effect. Many film themes tell the audience what to feel. The five-note phrase instead asks a question.

A complete melody encourages passive listening because it arrives with its own beginning, middle and end. An unfinished phrase encourages participation. Even if viewers cannot consciously identify the mechanism, they sense that the pattern wants a continuation. The aliens do not simply broadcast information; they engage in turn-taking.

That is why the exchange feels less like a performance and more like a conversation. The audience hears a call and then waits, alongside the human characters, to discover whether anything will answer.

## A Signal That Remains Mysterious

The lasting brilliance of the motif is that it never entirely stops feeling alien, even after decades of familiarity. Many famous film themes become comfortable through repetition. The *Close Encounters* phrase remains slightly unsettling because it never fully reveals its purpose.

The motif is memorable enough to recognise instantly, yet abstract enough to resist interpretation.
