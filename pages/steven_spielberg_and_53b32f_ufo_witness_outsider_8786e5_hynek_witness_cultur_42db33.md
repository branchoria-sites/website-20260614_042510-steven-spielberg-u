## Introduction

The outsider status of [Roy Neary]({{ 'roy-neary/' | relative_url }}) in *[Close Encounters]({{ 'close-encounters/' | relative_url }}) of the Third Kind* did not emerge solely from Steven Spielberg’s imagination. It drew heavily on the witness culture described by astronomer and UFO researcher J. Allen Hynek, whose work shaped both the film’s title and its understanding of what it meant to report an extraordinary sighting. Hynek became convinced that the most important aspect of the UFO question was not simply the objects being reported but the social position of the people reporting them. Again and again, he encountered witnesses who believed they had seen something unusual yet feared ridicule, disbelief or professional embarrassment.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://catalog.afi.com/Film/67160-CLOSE-ENCOUNTERS-OF-THE-THIRD-KIND" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalog.afi.com">[AFI Catalog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalog.afi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AFI CatalogClose Encounters of the Third Kind (1977) - AFI CatalogA settlement resulted in Columbia purchasing the rights to Hynek&#x27;s book...</span></span></span>

That concern helps explain why Roy is portrayed less as an action hero than as a socially exposed individual. Before Spielberg’s aliens arrive as a spectacle, the film presents the witness experience as a crisis of [credibility]({{ 'credibility/' | relative_url }}). In that respect, *Close Encounters* translated a real research culture into drama: Hynek studied people whose testimony placed them at odds with accepted reality, while Spielberg turned that tension into the emotional core of his story.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://catalog.afi.com/Film/67160-CLOSE-ENCOUNTERS-OF-THE-THIRD-KIND" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalog.afi.com">[AFI Catalog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalog.afi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AFI CatalogClose Encounters of the Third Kind (1977) - AFI CatalogA settlement resulted in Columbia purchasing the rights to Hynek&#x27;s book...</span></span></span>

## Hynek's Witness Classifications

Hynek is best known for creating the classification system that gave Spielberg his title. In *The UFO Experience* (1972), he organised reports into categories ranging from distant observations to increasingly close encounters. His purpose was not merely to label sightings but to create a framework that treated witness testimony as something that could be examined systematically rather than dismissed out of hand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Allen_Hynek" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">J. Allen Hynek</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">J. Allen Hynek</span></span></span>

The classification system reflected a broader idea: [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) occupied different positions relative to an event, and the quality of testimony changed with proximity. Hynek argued that reports made at close range deserved careful attention because opportunities for simple misidentification were reduced. This approach encouraged investigators to focus on the human experience of witnessing as well as on the reported phenomenon itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

For Spielberg, this framework offered more than a title. It provided a narrative model. Roy Neary is not a scientist, military officer or government expert. He is an ordinary witness whose life is transformed because he was present at an unusual event. That emphasis mirrors Hynek’s long-standing interest in everyday observers rather than elite authorities as the central figures in UFO reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_Encounters_of_the_Third_Kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 18, 2026 — The film depicts the interconnected stories of Roy Neary (Dreyfuss), an everyday blue-collar worker in Indiana whose lif...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

## Ridicule as a UFO Culture Problem

One of Hynek’s most persistent complaints was the role of ridicule in discussions of UFO reports. After years working with Air Force investigations, he became increasingly critical of what he saw as a tendency to dismiss witnesses before examining their claims. His best-known statement on the subject argued that “ridicule is not part of the scientific method” and that reports from apparently reliable observers created an obligation for serious inquiry rather than mockery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Allen_Hynek" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">J. Allen Hynek</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">J. Allen Hynek</span></span></span>

This concern was not abstract. Hynek repeatedly pointed to pilots, police officers and other trained observers whose reports complicated the assumption that all witnesses were confused, dishonest or attention-seeking. He became frustrated by institutional habits that appeared to seek explanations first and investigations second.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

The culture of ridicule also had practical consequences. Contemporary and historical discussions of UFO reporting frequently note that witnesses may stay silent because they fear professional or social repercussions. Air Force reporting debates from the [Cold War]({{ 'cold-war/' | relative_url }}) era acknowledged concerns that personnel were reluctant to report sightings because of embarrassment or ridicule.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark%3A/67531/metadc993849/m2/1/high_res_d/76-52SP_1976march9.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digital.library.unt.edu">[UNT Digital Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digital.library.unt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNT Digital LibraryCRS CRSeptember 22, 2017 — 9 Mar 1976 — report UFO sightings &#x27;for fear of ridicule and arranged for a new directive to...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 22, 2017</span></span></span>

For Hynek, therefore, the UFO witness occupied a peculiar social position. A person could be convinced that something significant had happened and yet know that speaking openly risked becoming the object of suspicion or humour. That tension became one of the defining features of UFO culture and a crucial influence on how Spielberg framed his protagonist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_Encounters_of_the_Third_Kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 18, 2026 — The film depicts the interconnected stories of Roy Neary (Dreyfuss), an everyday blue-collar worker in Indiana whose lif...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

## How Spielberg Turns Witness Stigma into Drama

Roy Neary embodies many of the pressures Hynek associated with UFO witnesses. After his sighting, Roy gains certainty but loses credibility. He cannot communicate his experience in a way that satisfies his family or fits ordinary social expectations. The result is isolation rather than validation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_Encounters_of_the_Third_Kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 18, 2026 — The film depicts the interconnected stories of Roy Neary (Dreyfuss), an everyday blue-collar worker in Indiana whose lif...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

What makes this significant is that Spielberg largely avoids presenting Roy as a fraud, a fantasist or a conventional eccentric. Instead, the audience is allowed to know that Roy really has encountered something extraordinary. The dramatic tension comes from watching a truthful witness appear irrational to everyone around him. This structure closely resembles Hynek’s repeated argument that the witness problem often emerged before the evidence problem. Social judgement arrived first.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_Encounters_of_the_Third_Kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 18, 2026 — The film depicts the interconnected stories of Roy Neary (Dreyfuss), an everyday blue-collar worker in Indiana whose lif...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

The film also adopts Hynek’s interest in ordinary people as repositories of unusual knowledge. Spielberg had initially explored projects centred on people who believed they had encountered UFOs, and *Close Encounters* retained that focus even after becoming a large-scale science-fiction production.
