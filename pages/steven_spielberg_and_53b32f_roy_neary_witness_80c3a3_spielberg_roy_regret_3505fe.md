## Introduction

The ending of *[Close Encounters]({{ 'close-encounters/' | relative_url }}) of the Third Kind* remains one of the most celebrated moments in UFO cinema: [Roy Neary]({{ 'roy-neary/' | relative_url }}) boards an alien craft and leaves Earth behind. Yet Steven Spielberg later admitted that this was the aspect of the film he would most question. What once seemed to him a triumphant act of transcendence came to look different after he became a husband and father. His change of perspective did not erase the ending, but it transformed how many viewers—and Spielberg himself—understand Roy as a UFO witness. Rather than a pure hero rewarded for seeking the unknown, Roy became a more morally complicated figure whose quest for cosmic truth comes at a profound personal cost. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/steven-spielberg-would-make-one-change-to-close-encounters-if-making-the-film-today" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: syfy.com">[SYFY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">syfy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Steven Spielberg Would Make This One Change to Close</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Steven Spielberg Would Make This One Change to Close...May 14, 2026 — &quot;I would never have made Close Encounters the way I made it in...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 14, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why the Original Ending Rewards Roy

Within the logic of the film, Roy's departure is presented as the culmination of an extraordinary calling. After his encounter with the UFOs, he becomes consumed by visions he cannot explain. The narrative repeatedly validates his obsession: the mysterious image in his mind proves real, the government cover-up exists, and the aliens are benevolent. By the final sequence at [Devils Tower]({{ 'devils-tower/' | relative_url }}), Roy's persistence has been vindicated.

For the younger Spielberg, this ending reflected the emotional priorities that shaped the film. Roy's decision is framed as a leap into wonder and discovery. The universe has opened itself to him, and he accepts the invitation. The closing images encourage the audience to share his sense of awe rather than dwell on the practical consequences for the family left behind. Critics and audiences often experienced the finale as a euphoric release, the ultimate reward for a witness who followed the truth despite ridicule and social isolation. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_Encounters_of_the_Third_Kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span></span></span>

What makes the ending unusual is that the film does not punish Roy for abandoning ordinary life. Instead, it treats his departure as a kind of ascension. That narrative choice helped define the UFO witness archetype in popular culture: the person who becomes so transformed by contact with the unknown that everyday obligations no longer seem sufficient.

## How Family Changes the Moral Reading

Spielberg's later comments reveal how dramatically his own interpretation shifted. Reflecting on the film years after becoming a parent, he said he would never make *Close Encounters* in quite the same way because he now had a family he would never leave. Looking back, he described Roy's decision as something connected to the perspective of his younger self, calling it "the privilege of youth." <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/steven-spielberg-would-make-one-change-to-close-encounters-if-making-the-film-today" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: syfy.com">[SYFY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">syfy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Steven Spielberg Would Make This One Change to Close</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Steven Spielberg Would Make This One Change to Close...May 14, 2026 — &quot;I would never have made Close Encounters the way I made it in...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 14, 2026</span></span></span>

This retrospective regret matters because it changes the emotional centre of the ending. Once family responsibility becomes part of the equation, Roy's final choice no longer looks purely liberating. It raises difficult questions:

His wife becomes frightened and exhausted, and his children witness their father's increasingly erratic behaviour. ## Roy as Awe and Selfishness Together

Spielberg's later regret is especially significant because it does not invalidate Roy's experience. The film still insists that Roy is correct. He really has encountered something extraordinary. The aliens are real. His visions are meaningful. His obsession is not a delusion.

That creates a tension rarely found in conventional science-fiction stories. Roy is both justified and destructive. The same certainty that allows him to discover the truth also damages the people closest to him. Spielberg's later comments effectively invite audiences to hold both ideas at once. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/steven-spielberg-would-make-one-change-to-close-encounters-if-making-the-film-today" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: syfy.com">[SYFY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">syfy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Steven Spielberg Would Make This One Change to Close</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Steven Spielberg Would Make This One Change to Close...May 14, 2026 — &quot;I would never have made Close Encounters the way I made it in...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 14, 2026</span></span></span>

This duality has become central to modern interpretations of the character. Roy is no longer simply the dreamer who follows a cosmic summons. He is also a man whose pursuit of transcendence blinds him to obligations on Earth. His journey embodies both the attraction and the danger of obsession.

Some commentators have connected this evolution in Spielberg's thinking to broader themes that emerged throughout his career. Many of his later films place greater emphasis on parents, children and fractured families, suggesting that his understanding of responsibility changed as his own life changed. ## The Ending Spielberg Later Questioned

Spielberg never removed Roy's departure from the film, even after publicly expressing reservations about it. That decision is revealing. Rather than revising the story to fit his later beliefs, he allowed the ending to stand as evidence of who he was when he made it.

As a result, *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* occupies a distinctive place within Spielberg's UFO work. Its climactic act of wonder remains emotionally powerful, but it is now inseparable from the questions Spielberg later raised about family, commitment and responsibility. The ending still celebrates humanity's desire to reach beyond the known. It simply does so with a moral ambiguity that became clearer over time.

For readers considering Roy Neary as the archetypal UFO witness, Spielberg's later regret is essential. It turns Roy from a straightforward seeker of truth into a figure who embodies two competing impulses: the longing to follow the extraordinary wherever it leads, and the obligations that tie people to those who depend on them.
