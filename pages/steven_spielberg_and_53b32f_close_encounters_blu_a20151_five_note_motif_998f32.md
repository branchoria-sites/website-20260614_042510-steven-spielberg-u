## Introduction

One of the most influential ideas in *[Close Encounters]({{ 'close-encounters/' | relative_url }}) of the Third Kind* is also one of its simplest: five musical notes. Rather than making first contact through weapons, translated speech, or advanced technology, Steven Spielberg and composer John Williams imagined communication beginning with a short, repeatable musical phrase. The result is the film’s famous five-note motif, a signal that functions as a shared language between humans and extraterrestrials. ## Why the Motif Works as a Simple Shared Code

The brilliance of the five-note sequence lies in its simplicity. A spoken language depends on culture, vocabulary, and shared meanings. A short pattern of pitches does not. Anyone capable of recognising patterns can identify whether the sequence has been repeated correctly.

John Williams later explained that Spielberg insisted the communication signal should consist of only [five notes]({{ 'five-notes/' | relative_url }}). Williams reportedly created hundreds of possible combinations before the final version was selected. ## How Call and Response Replaces Military Conflict

Many earlier alien-invasion stories treated contact as an immediate security problem. *Close Encounters* deliberately chooses a different path. During the climactic exchange at [Devils Tower]({{ 'devils-tower/' | relative_url }}), scientists and the alien craft engage in a musical call-and-response sequence. One side sends the phrase; the other answers.

The structure resembles a conversation. Each repetition confirms that the message has been received and understood. As the exchanges become more elaborate, tension comes not from the threat of attack but from the possibility of successful communication. The audience watches the same basic dramatic question that drives any dialogue: will the other side answer?<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nkykqyMEarA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: youtube.com">[YouTube]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">youtube.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close Encounters of the Third Kind: Communicating with musicHumans and aliens try to communicate with each other with music. This approach fundamentally changes the emotional logic of the scene. The military presence remains in the background, but it does not determine the outcome. The decisive action belongs to musicians, scientists, and communicators. The UFO is not defeated, escaped, or destroyed. Instead, it participates.

The five-note phrase therefore performs a narrative function as well as a musical one. It gives the film a mechanism for resolving uncertainty without violence. Spielberg's aliens become intelligible because they respond according to recognisable rules. Every successful exchange reduces fear and increases mutual recognition.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://houstonsymphony.org/the-sound-of-cinema-john-williams-steven-spielbergs-legendary-partnership/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: houstonsymphony.org">[Houston Symphony]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">houstonsymphony.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the sound of cinema john williams steven spielbergs legendary partnership</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Houston SymphonyJohn Williams &amp; Steven Spielberg&#x27;s Legendary Partnership21 Apr 2025 — In 1977, Close Encounters of the Third Kind introdu...</span></span></span>

## Why Music Makes the Ending Feel Credible

The ending asks viewers to accept an extraordinary idea: that humanity could communicate with a non-human intelligence during a first encounter. Music helps make that idea feel plausible because it occupies a space between mathematics and emotion.

On one level, the motif is technical. The notes can be converted into electronic signals, colours, and frequencies. The film's communication system links sound, light, and pattern recognition, suggesting a framework that an advanced civilisation might also understand.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://thelistenersclub.com/2020/01/03/john-williams-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-a-cinematic-tone-poem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelistenersclub.com">[The Listeners&#x27; Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelistenersclub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">john williams close encounters of the third kind a cinematic tone poem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Listeners&#x27; ClubJohn Williams&#x27; “Close Encounters of the Third Kind”3 Jan 2020 — Spielberg described the score&#x27;s iconic five-note “Comm...</span></span></span>

On another level, music is deeply emotional. Humans routinely use music to communicate feelings that are difficult to express in words. By choosing music rather than spoken dialogue, Spielberg avoids the problem of instant translation while preserving emotional connection. The audience does not need subtitles to understand that the exchange is friendly, curious, and increasingly joyful.

Williams reinforced this effect through the structure of the motif itself. Analysts of the score have noted that the phrase feels unfinished, almost as if it is waiting for a reply. That quality makes the audience anticipate a response every time it is heard. The motif becomes a question as much as a statement.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://thelistenersclub.com/2020/01/03/john-williams-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-a-cinematic-tone-poem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelistenersclub.com">[The Listeners&#x27; Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelistenersclub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">john williams close encounters of the third kind a cinematic tone poem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Listeners&#x27; ClubJohn Williams&#x27; “Close Encounters of the Third Kind”3 Jan 2020 — Spielberg described the score&#x27;s iconic five-note “Comm...</span></span></span>

Because the audience experiences the signal repeatedly throughout the film, the final exchange feels earned rather than arbitrary. What begins as a mysterious sound reported by [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) eventually becomes the key that unlocks contact. ## The Lasting Importance of the Five Notes

The five-note motif became one of the most recognisable musical ideas in science-fiction cinema, but its importance extends beyond memorability. It embodies Spielberg’s central UFO vision: the unknown is not necessarily hostile, and understanding begins with communication.

The motif turns first contact into a shared act of listening. Humans do not conquer the aliens, and the aliens do not dominate humanity. Instead, both sides participate in a common system of signals. In a film filled with enormous spacecraft and spectacular visual effects, the crucial breakthrough arrives through a sequence simple enough to be whistled.

That choice helped define *Close Encounters* as Spielberg’s foundational UFO film. Its most important message is not hidden in a government file or revealed by a scientific discovery.
