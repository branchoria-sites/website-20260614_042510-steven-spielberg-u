## Introduction

[Devils Tower]({{ 'devils-tower/' | relative_url }}) works as a UFO signal in *[Close Encounters]({{ 'close-encounters/' | relative_url }}) of the Third Kind* because it looks less like a conventional mountain than a piece of information. Before anyone in the film knows its name, the landmark functions as a repeated visual code: a simple shape that can be remembered, drawn and recognised instantly. Steven Spielberg’s choice succeeds because Devils Tower combines three unusual qualities at once. It has a singular silhouette, a broad flat summit that seems designed for arrival, and a geological structure so striking that it can feel intentional even when viewers know it is natural. ## The Silhouette Before the Name

One of the film’s most effective mechanisms is that audiences encounter the shape before they encounter the place. [Roy Neary]({{ 'roy-neary/' | relative_url }}) and other [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) become obsessed with an image they cannot identify. The landmark exists first as a mental imprint and only later as a real location in Wyoming. The AFI plot summary describes this progression as a recurring mountain form that ultimately leads characters to Devils Tower. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/MovieDetails/67160?cxt=filmography" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalog.afi.com">[AFI Catalog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalog.afi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Movie Details</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AFI CatalogClose Encounters of the Third Kind (1977) - AFI CatalogReviewing a map of a Wyoming mountain called Devil&#x27;s Tower, military pe...</span></span></span>

That narrative device only works if the image is unmistakable. Devils Tower is unusually suited to this role because its outline can be recognised from almost any angle. Unlike a mountain range, which requires context, the Tower reads as a single object. It rises abruptly from the surrounding landscape rather than blending into it, creating a shape that can be reduced to a simple sketch without losing its identity. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.intermountainhistories.org/items/show/226" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intermountainhistories.org">[Intermountain Histories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intermountainhistories.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Also visible is the Belle Fourche...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Why the Flat Summit Feels Like an Invitation

The top of Devils Tower contributes as much to its symbolic power as its height.

Many famous mountains culminate in peaks, ridges or jagged summits. Devils Tower instead presents a broad, level top resting on nearly vertical sides. Seen from a distance, it resembles a platform more than a peak. This creates a subtle psychological effect: viewers can imagine something arriving there.

The film never needs to state this directly. The shape does the work. A pointed mountain suggests climbing and conquest. A flat-topped monolith suggests rendezvous. The summit appears less like a natural endpoint and more like a designated location.

This visual logic helps explain why the site feels credible as the endpoint of extraterrestrial communication. The audience does not merely accept that aliens chose Devils Tower; the mountain’s geometry makes the choice seem intuitive. ## Geology That Feels Improbable

The deepest reason Devils Tower functions as a UFO signal is that its geology already produces the sensation of design.

Geologists identify the Tower as an igneous intrusion formed when magma cooled and hardened underground. As that material contracted during cooling, it fractured into long polygonal columns, creating the vertical grooves that define the monument today. The resulting columnar jointing is one of the most spectacular examples of the phenomenon anywhere in the world. * The sides rise almost vertically for hundreds of feet.
* The overall form resembles a single carved object rather than an eroded landscape.

The film exploits this tension between explanation and appearance. Viewers understand that Devils Tower is a real geological formation, yet its geometry feels so orderly that it can plausibly serve as a beacon, marker or landing site within the story’s logic.

Importantly, Spielberg does not present the Tower as alien technology. The location remains recognisably natural. Its effectiveness comes from occupying an unusual middle ground: it looks real enough to be credible and strange enough to seem chosen.

## A Signal Hidden in Plain Sight

Most fictional UFO landing sites are invented landscapes. Devils Tower achieves something more powerful because it already exists.

Its silhouette is memorable before it is identified. Its summit suggests a meeting place. Its columned walls look improbably structured while remaining scientifically explicable. Together, these characteristics allow the monument to function as a signal without emitting anything at all.

In *Close Encounters*, the message is not a broadcast from space. It is a shape.
